What Is Hypothesis Testing?

At its heart, hypothesis testing is a structured way of making decisions under uncertainty. You have some claim about the world — maybe a new drug lowers blood pressure, or a factory machine is producing items that are too heavy — and you want to know: is this claim actually true, or could the data you are seeing just be due to random chance?

The Core Idea

We start by assuming nothing has changed (this is called the null hypothesis). We then collect data and ask: "How likely would this data be if the null hypothesis were true?" If the answer is "very unlikely," we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that something real is going on.

Think of it like a courtroom: the defendant is assumed innocent until proven guilty. The null hypothesis is innocence. The data is the evidence. The question is whether the evidence is strong enough to overturn the assumption.

A Simple Real-World Analogy

Imagine a coin. You suspect it is biased towards heads. You flip it 100 times and get 70 heads. Is that proof the coin is biased, or could you get 70 heads just by luck with a fair coin?

Hypothesis testing gives you a precise, mathematically grounded answer to exactly that type of question. It tells you the probability of getting 70 or more heads if the coin were truly fair — and if that probability is small enough, you conclude the coin is indeed biased. This logic applies to medicine, engineering, business, psychology, and virtually every field that uses data.

The Two Hypotheses: Null and Alternative

Every hypothesis test begins with two competing statements about the world. Understanding these clearly is the single most important foundation of the entire topic.

The Null Hypothesis (H₀) — "Nothing New Is Happening"

The null hypothesis is the default position — the status quo. It typically states that there is no effect, no difference, or no change. For example: "This drug has no effect on blood pressure" or "The average weight of cereal boxes from this machine is exactly 500g." We assume H₀ is true until the data gives us strong evidence against it. The null hypothesis always contains an equality: =, ≤, or ≥.

The Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ) — "Something Has Changed"

The alternative hypothesis is what you actually want to test — the claim you are trying to find evidence for. For example: "This drug lowers blood pressure" or "The machine is over-filling the cereal boxes." The alternative hypothesis uses strict inequalities: ≠, <, or >. The direction of your alternative hypothesis determines what kind of test you run (more on this shortly).

Three Common Formulations

Two-Tailed Test

H₀: μ = 500g
H₁: μ ≠ 500g

"The mean is different from 500g — could be higher or lower."

Right-Tailed Test

H₀: μ ≤ 500g
H₁: μ > 500g

"The mean is greater than 500g."

Left-Tailed Test

H₀: μ ≥ 500g
H₁: μ < 500g

"The mean is less than 500g."

Significance Level (α): Setting the Bar

Before you collect any data, you need to decide how much evidence is "enough" to reject the null hypothesis. This threshold is called the significance level, denoted by the Greek letter α (alpha).

What Does α Actually Mean?

The significance level α is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true. In other words, it is the probability of making a false alarm — concluding there is an effect when there really is not one. This type of error is called a Type I Error.

α = 0.05 (5%)

Most common in social sciences and medicine. Used by default unless stated otherwise.

α = 0.01 (1%)

Used when the cost of a false alarm is high (e.g., pharmaceutical approval).

α = 0.10 (10%)

Used in exploratory research where you are willing to take more risk.

The Intuition Behind α

Think of α as setting the burden of proof. A smaller α means you demand stronger evidence before concluding the null hypothesis is false. A larger α means you are more easily convinced.

Crucially, α is chosen before looking at any data — not after. Choosing α after seeing the data would be like changing the rules of a game after you see the results. It undermines the integrity of the entire procedure.

Always state your significance level before conducting the test.

This is a fundamental rule of statistical ethics and scientific reproducibility.

The p-Value: The Heart of the Decision

Once you have collected your data and computed a test statistic, you need a way to decide whether your result is surprising enough to reject H₀. This is where the p-value comes in — it is arguably the most important (and most misunderstood) number in statistics.

The p-value is the probability of observing a test result as extreme as — or more extreme than — the one you got, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

Plain English Translation

The p-value answers this question: "If H₀ were actually true, how often would I get data this extreme just by chance?"

Small p-value (e.g., p = 0.002)

This data would be very rare if H₀ were true. Strong evidence against H₀. → Likely reject H₀.

Large p-value (e.g., p = 0.45)

This data is quite plausible if H₀ were true. Weak evidence against H₀. → Do not reject H₀.

Decision rule: if p ≤ α, reject H₀. If p > α, fail to reject H₀.

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

The p-value is widely misunderstood, even by experienced researchers. Here are the most important things the p-value is NOT. Tap each one to see why.

❌ It is NOT the probability that H₀ is true.
The p-value assumes H₀ is true and asks how surprising the data is. It cannot also tell you how likely H₀ is.
❌ It is NOT the probability that the result occurred by chance.
It is a probability about the data given H₀ — not a probability that "chance did it".
❌ A small p-value does NOT mean the effect is large or practically important.
With a huge sample, tiny differences can give tiny p-values.
❌ "Failing to reject H₀" does NOT mean H₀ is true.
It just means you did not find enough evidence against it.

The p-value is purely about the data given H₀. It says nothing directly about the truth of H₀.

Walk through the five steps

The z-Test: When Population Standard Deviation Is Known

The one-sample z-test is the most fundamental hypothesis test. You use it when you are testing a claim about a population mean (μ) and you know the population standard deviation (σ). Thanks to the Central Limit Theorem, even if the population is not perfectly normal, the z-test is valid for large samples (n ≥ 30).

The z-Test Statistic Formula

z = x̄ − μ₀σ / √n
  • x̄ = sample mean
  • μ₀ = hypothesised population mean (from H₀)
  • σ = known population standard deviation
  • n = sample size

This formula is exactly what you recognise from standardisation — it converts the sample mean into a z-score on the standard normal distribution.

Type I and Type II Error: When Tests Go Wrong

No hypothesis test is perfect. Because we are making decisions based on sample data — and samples are subject to random variation — we can sometimes arrive at the wrong conclusion. There are exactly two ways a hypothesis test can be wrong, and understanding them is critical.

Decision ↓ / Reality →H₀ is trueH₀ is false
Reject H₀Type I error (false positive), probability αCorrect (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H₀CorrectType II error (false negative), probability β

Type I Error (False Positive) — α

Definition: Rejecting H₀ when H₀ is actually true.

Real-world example: A doctor concludes a patient has a disease (rejects H₀: "no disease") when the patient is actually healthy. This is a false alarm.

Probability: The probability of a Type I error is exactly α — the significance level you set. By choosing α = 0.05, you accept a 5% risk of a false alarm whenever H₀ is true.

How to reduce it: Use a smaller α (e.g., 0.01). But this comes at a cost...

Type II Error (False Negative) — β

Definition: Failing to reject H₀ when H₀ is actually false.

Real-world example: A doctor concludes the patient is healthy (fails to reject H₀) when the patient actually has the disease. A missed diagnosis.

Probability: The probability of a Type II error is denoted β (beta). It depends on α, sample size, and the true effect size.

How to reduce it: Increase sample size, or use a larger α. Note the trade-off: decreasing α (reducing Type I errors) tends to increase β (increasing Type II errors).

Power of a Test = 1 − β

The power of a hypothesis test is the probability of correctly rejecting H₀ when H₀ is false — i.e., the probability of detecting a real effect. Power = 1 − β. A power of 0.80 (80%) is generally considered adequate for most research. Higher power requires a larger sample size. Researchers often perform a power analysis before collecting data to determine the sample size needed to achieve adequate power.

The t-Test: When σ Is Unknown

In practice, you almost never know the true population standard deviation σ. Instead, you estimate it using the sample standard deviation s. When you do this, the standardised test statistic no longer follows a normal distribution — it follows a t-distribution, which has heavier tails (more uncertainty) than the normal distribution. The t-distribution has a parameter called degrees of freedom (df), which controls how heavy the tails are. For a one-sample t-test, df = n − 1.

The t-Test Statistic

t = x̄ − μ₀s / √n  ,   df = n − 1
  • x̄ = sample mean
  • μ₀ = hypothesised mean (from H₀)
  • s = sample standard deviation
  • n = sample size
  • df = n − 1

The formula looks identical to the z-test formula, but you use s instead of σ, and you compare the result to a t-table instead of a z-table.

When to Use z vs. t

Use z-test when:

  • σ is known (rare in practice)
  • n ≥ 30 and σ is known

Use t-test when:

  • σ is unknown (very common)
  • n is small (n < 30)
  • The population is approximately normal

As n increases, the t-distribution approaches the normal distribution. For very large n (say, n > 100), the t and z critical values become nearly identical. This is why many textbooks say "use z for large n, t for small n" — though in practice, using the t-distribution is always safer when σ is unknown.

Connecting Hypothesis Tests to Confidence Intervals

You have already studied confidence intervals (CIs) in a previous unit. It turns out that hypothesis tests and confidence intervals are two sides of the same coin. Understanding their connection gives you a much deeper intuition for both.

The Deep Connection

A 95% confidence interval gives you a range of plausible values for the population parameter. A two-tailed hypothesis test at α = 0.05 rejects H₀ if the hypothesised value falls outside the 95% confidence interval. In other words:

  • If μ₀ falls inside the 95% CI → fail to reject H₀ at α = 0.05
  • If μ₀ falls outside the 95% CI → reject H₀ at α = 0.05

This duality means that a confidence interval actually gives you more information than a hypothesis test — it tells you not just whether to reject H₀, but also what values are plausible for the parameter. Many statisticians now advocate reporting confidence intervals alongside (or instead of) p-values.

Example: Returning to the Cereal Boxes

Recall from Example 1: x̄ = 496g, σ = 12g, n = 36, and we rejected H₀: μ = 500g at α = 0.05. The 95% confidence interval for μ is:

CI = x̄ ± z0.025 · σ√n = 496 ± 1.96 · 12√36 = 496 ± 3.92

This gives a 95% CI of (492.08g, 499.92g). Notice that the hypothesised value of 500g is not inside this interval — consistent with our earlier decision to reject H₀. The CI also tells us something the p-value did not: the true mean is likely between 492g and 500g, which helps the manufacturer understand the severity of the under-filling issue.

Key Concept Summary & Common Pitfalls

Before we wrap up, let us consolidate the most important ideas from this entire guide and flag the most common mistakes students make in hypothesis testing. Use this as a revision checklist.

Pitfall 1: Confusing "Fail to Reject" with "Accept H₀"

Not rejecting H₀ does NOT mean H₀ is true. It only means the evidence was insufficient. You never "prove" H₀ — you only fail to disprove it.

Pitfall 2: p-Hacking and Choosing α After Seeing Data

Always set α before collecting data. Running multiple tests and reporting only the significant ones (p-hacking) inflates the Type I error rate far above α and is a serious breach of scientific integrity.

Pitfall 3: Statistical vs. Practical Significance

With a very large sample, even a tiny, unimportant difference can be statistically significant (very small p-value). Always ask: "Is this result meaningful in practice?" A 0.001°C temperature change may be statistically significant but practically irrelevant.

Pitfall 4: Violating Test Assumptions

The z-test assumes σ is known and n is large (or the population is normal). The t-test assumes approximate normality or large n. Always check your assumptions before running any test.

Statistical vs. practical significance

The true difference is fixed at a trivial 0.5 points (σ = 10). Only the sample size changes.

The Chi-Square Test: Introduction

So far, we have tested claims about means (averages). But what if your data is not numbers — what if it is categories? For example: favourite color, yes/no answers, blood type.

The Chi-Square (χ²) test is designed for categorical data. Think of it like checking whether the pieces in a bag are arranged the way we expect.

Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test

Tests whether one categorical variable matches an expected distribution.

Example: Are birthday months spread evenly across the year?

Chi-Square Test of Independence

Tests whether two categorical variables are related to each other.

Example: Is favorite color related to class year?

χ² is pronounced "kai-squared".

The Chi-Square Statistic: The Core Formula (Simplified)

The chi-square statistic measures how far your observed data is from what you expected. The bigger the gap, the bigger the value, and the more suspicious we are of the null hypothesis.

χ² = Σ (Observed − Expected)²Expected

Observed (O)

What you actually counted in your data

Expected (E)

What you would expect if the null hypothesis were true

(O − E)²

Squaring makes all differences positive and punishes big gaps more

Dividing by E

Scales the gap relative to how big E is

If χ² ≈ 0, your data matches expectations perfectly. If χ² is large, something is off. Degrees of freedom: df = number of categories − 1 for goodness of fit.

Chi-Square Goodness of Fit Test: Does Reality Match the Claim?

Sometimes a company makes a claim like "our customers are split equally across all regions" or "our product sells in these exact proportions." The Goodness of Fit test helps you check: is that claim actually true?

Think of it like a vending machine that is supposed to give out 4 snack types equally. If you check 100 buys and one snack shows up way more than the others, you would wonder: is something wrong, or is it just luck? The Goodness of Fit test gives you a scientific answer.

When do you use it?

  • You have one category to look at, like product type, region, or day of the week
  • Someone has made a claim about how the data should be split
  • You want to check if the real data matches that claim

How does it work?

You compare what you actually saw with what you expected based on the claim. For each category, you ask: how far off is the real count from the expected count? Then you add up all those gaps to get the chi-square value. The bigger the value, the more the data disagrees with the claim.

Equal split claimed

Example: "All 4 regions help equally with sales, so each one gets 25%."

Expected count = total ÷ number of categories

Unequal split claimed

Example: "North = 40%, South = 30%, East = 20%, West = 10%."

Expected count = total × claimed share

One simple formula

E = n × p

Expected count = total observations × the claimed share for that category.

Degrees of freedom

df = number of categories − 1. So if you have 4 categories, df = 3.

Remember: H₀ says the claim is correct. A large χ² means the data does not match the claim, so we start to doubt H₀.

Chi-Square: Assumptions, Limitations & Common Pitfalls

Assumptions (must be met before running the test)

Categorical data

Chi-square only works with counts/frequencies, not means or percentages directly.

Independence of observations

Each person/item should only appear once in the table.

Expected frequency rule

Every expected cell count (E) should be at least 5. If not, the chi-square approximation breaks down. (Tip: if some E values are below 5, consider combining categories or using Fisher's Exact Test instead.)

Random sampling

Data should come from a random sample.

Common Pitfalls

❌ Using raw percentages instead of counts — always use actual counts (frequencies) in the table.

❌ Confusing "no relationship" with "proven independence" — failing to reject H₀ does NOT prove the variables are independent, just that we don't have enough evidence.

❌ Ignoring small expected counts — if E < 5 in any cell, your result may be unreliable.

❌ Thinking chi-square tells you HOW STRONG the relationship is — it only tells you IF a relationship exists.

Quick Reference

Goodness of Fit: df = k − 1 (k = number of categories)

Test of Independence: df = (r − 1)(c − 1) (r = rows, c = columns)

Always use COUNTS, not percentages

ANOVA: Comparing More Than Two Groups

Imagine you are a manager testing whether three different training programs lead to different employee performance scores. You could run multiple t-tests — but that increases your chance of making an error. ANOVA does it all in one go, safely.

ANOVA = Analysis of Variance. Despite the name, it is actually used to compare means — not variances. The name comes from the method it uses under the hood.

When do you use ANOVA?

  • You have one numerical outcome variable (e.g. test score, sales, time)
  • You have one categorical grouping variable with 3 or more groups (e.g. training method A, B, C)
  • You want to know: do at least some of these groups have different means?

ANOVA works by comparing two things:

  1. How much do scores vary between groups? Are the group averages far apart?
  2. How much do scores vary within groups? Are individual scores spread out inside each group?

If the between-group variation is much larger than the within-group variation, something real is going on — the groups are genuinely different.

Hypotheses

  • H₀: All group means are equal (μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ = ...)
  • H₁: At least one group mean is different

Quick note: ANOVA tells you that at least one group is different — but not which one. For that, you need a follow-up test called a Post-Hoc test (like Tukey's test). We will not go into that here, but good to know it exists.

The test statistic: F-ratio

F = Variation between groupsVariation within groups

Plain English: If F is close to 1, the groups are similar. If F is large, at least one group stands out.

Formula section

F = MSbetween / MSwithin
MSbetween = SSbetween / dfbetween    MSwithin = SSwithin / dfwithin

SS_between — Between-Group Sum of Squares

This measures how far each group's average is from the overall average. If the groups have very different means, this number will be large.

Formula: SS_between = Σ nᵢ × (x̄ᵢ − x̄_grand)²

Plain English: For each group, find the gap between that group's mean and the grand mean. Square it, multiply by the group size, then add them all up.

SS_within — Within-Group Sum of Squares

This measures how spread out the individual scores are inside each group. If people in the same group have very different scores, this number will be large.

Formula: SS_within = Σ Σ (xᵢⱼ − x̄ᵢ)²

Plain English: For every single data point, find the gap between that score and its group's mean. Square it, then add them all up across all groups.

Degrees of freedom

df_between = number of groups − 1
df_within = total number of observations − number of groups

MS = SS / df. Plain English: MS just means "average variation". We divide by degrees of freedom to make the comparison fair regardless of sample size.

  • If F ≈ 1 → the groups are not very different (variation between ≈ variation within)
  • If F ≫ 1 → at least one group stands out as genuinely different

We compare F to a critical value from the F-table to make the final call.

The Two-Sample t-Test: Comparing Two Groups

So far, the t-test we covered compared ONE group to a fixed claim (e.g. "is our average different from 72?"). But what if you want to compare TWO groups against each other? That is exactly what the Two-Sample t-Test does.

Think of it like a race between two runners. You don't care how fast each one is in absolute terms — you just want to know: is one genuinely faster than the other, or is the difference just luck?

When do you use it?

  • You have TWO independent groups (e.g. Group A and Group B)
  • You want to compare their means
  • You do NOT know the population standard deviation (which is almost always the case in real life)
  • The two groups are separate — people in Group A are NOT the same people as in Group B

Hypotheses

  • H₀: The two group means are equal (μA = μB) — there is no real difference
  • H₁: The two group means are NOT equal (μA ≠ μB) — there IS a real difference

The Formula — broken down clearly

t = x̄A − x̄B√( sA²/nA + sB²/nB )

Break down every single piece

x̄A and x̄B

the sample means of Group A and Group B. "This is just the average of each group."

sA² and sB²

the sample variances of each group (variance = standard deviation squared). "This tells us how spread out the scores are in each group."

nA and nB

the number of observations in each group. "Bigger samples = more reliable estimates."

sA²/nA and sB²/nB

the variance contribution of each group. "We divide by n to account for sample size — larger samples reduce uncertainty."

√(sA²/nA + sB²/nB)

the Standard Error of the difference. "This is the key denominator. It measures how much the difference between the two means could vary just by chance."

t

the final ratio. "How many standard errors apart are the two group means? The bigger this number, the more evidence we have that the groups are truly different."

Degrees of freedom

df ≈ min(nA − 1, nB − 1)

"A simple approximation: use whichever group is smaller, subtract 1. This is slightly conservative but easy to apply."

Decision rule

"If |t| > t_critical → Reject H₀. The two groups are significantly different."

The two-sample t-test is the statistical engine behind A/B testing. When a company compares Version A vs Version B, this is the test running under the hood.

A/B Testing: Which Version Actually Works Better?

You have probably seen A/B testing in the real world without knowing it. When Netflix shows two different thumbnail designs to different users to see which one gets more clicks — that is A/B testing. When an e-commerce site tests two versions of a "Buy Now" button to see which one drives more sales — that is A/B testing too.

A/B testing is simply comparing two versions of something to see which one performs better. Version A is usually the current version (the control). Version B is the new version (the change you want to test). You split your audience randomly into two groups — one sees A, the other sees B — and then measure the outcome.

Version A (Control)

The existing version. This is your baseline. You already know how it performs.

Version B (Treatment)

The new version. You made one change — a new headline, a different colour, a new price. You want to know: did that change make a difference?

After running the test, you collect data from both groups. Then you run a hypothesis test — usually a two-sample t-test or a z-test — to check whether the difference in results between A and B is real, or just random chance.

Hypotheses

  • H₀: There is no difference between A and B (any difference is just random chance)
  • H₁: There IS a real difference between A and B

Key things to keep in mind

  • Only change ONE thing at a time — otherwise you won't know what caused the difference
  • Run the test long enough to collect sufficient data — stopping too early gives misleading results
  • Use random assignment — each user should be randomly assigned to A or B, not chosen by hand
  • Choose your significance level BEFORE running the test (usually α = 0.05)

A/B testing is just a two-sample hypothesis test in disguise. The "A" group and "B" group are your two samples. The question is always the same: is the difference between the two groups real, or could it have happened by chance?
